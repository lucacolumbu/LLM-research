{\LARGE\bfseries Mechanism-matched data scoring}\\[2pt]
{\large Compression predicts and accelerates the formation of induction heads}\\[2pt]
{\small Research summary, Luca Columbu, 2026. Full draft and code: \textcolor{red}{[link]}.}

\section{The question}
Pretraining data is selected with scalars that blend every capability
together: loss under a reference model, similarity to a target
distribution, redundancy. None of them can say which capability a
decision buys or sells, because none looks at what the model builds.
This project asks whether data can be scored against a specific
\emph{mechanism} instead: pick a circuit the model should learn, find
the cheapest program that performs the same computation, run it on each
document, and use its output as the data score.

\section{The idea}
An induction head finds the previous occurrence of the current token,
attends there, and copies what followed. That is also what the LZ77
compressor behind \texttt{gzip} does. So per-document compression
gain (how much shorter a document compresses than a shuffled copy of
itself) should predict when induction heads form, and selecting
documents by it should make them form sooner. A second compressor
quantity, the normalised compression distance between documents,
measures corpus diversity and should matter for something else.

\section{What was found}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gain predicts formation.} On synthetic copy tasks the
  training step at which the induction head forms is a monotone
  function of gain: Spearman $-0.98$ (attention-only), $-0.92$ (with
  MLPs), $-1.0$ on condition means, three seeds per condition, replicated
  at 3 layers and 8 heads. Where gain and the nominal repeated fraction
  disagree, formation follows gain. Weight decay changes nothing.
\item \textbf{The compressor measures the causal variable better than
  the data generator.} Selecting 20,000 of 60,000 mixed documents by gain
  forms the circuit at step 700 in every seed; a random subset at
  5,200 or never; an oracle that sees the hidden generating parameter at
  800--1,000. Gain tracks the realised copy length at $\rho=0.995$, the
  generating parameter at $0.60$, and a copy-length oracle picks a 97\%
  identical subset.
\item \textbf{The data picks the circuit variant.} With MLPs the model
  builds an induction circuit that keys on the token $k$ positions back,
  with $k$ fixed by the geometry of copies in the data and an exact
  accuracy ceiling. Plateaus at 0.66--0.76 that read as partial
  learning are completed lag-$k$ circuits. Tracing shows the lag is
  inherited from a positional copier the model builds before any
  induction head exists.
\item \textbf{Diversity is a different quantity.} Across 45 runs of a
  second task, gain and compression distance decorrelate; distance
  relates to generalisation across held-out name pairs, gain does not,
  and neither predicts when that task's circuit forms. That circuit
  turned out to be subject suppression plus MLP promotion, not the
  name-copying the task was designed for.
\item \textbf{A learned scorer repairs the blind spot.} Gain collapses
  when small vocabularies make accidental matches common. A small
  reference model that already has an induction head restores the
  prediction ($\rho$ from $-0.72$ to $-0.93$ pooled over 39 runs).
\end{itemize}

\section{Real data: Python code from The Stack}
2.5B tokens of Python, cut into 9.77M windows of 256 tokens. Two arms of
1.95M windows: greedy selection by gain with a diversity constraint
(compression distance at least 0.75 to already-selected documents) and a
random subset of equal size. A 4-layer, 8-head model trained on each for
491M tokens, three seeds; one seed per arm continued to 983M.

The selected arm forms the induction head 1.5--2$\times$ sooner in
attention and 2--3$\times$ sooner in behaviour, with no overlap across
seeds, and keeps a higher induction accuracy to the end. It pays 0.2
nats of validation loss, a cost that doubling the budget does not
remove: selecting the most repetitive fifth of a corpus narrows the
distribution. An in-context test on rare identifiers (loss at first
minus second occurrence, 4.2--4.9 nats in every run) shows that once
both circuits exist they are equally load-bearing in distribution.
Selection buys \emph{when} the circuit forms. A smaller local study
showed the two compressor quantities set the two axes of the trade-off:
gain buys the circuit, distance buys the distribution, and a diversity
constraint gets more of the first for less of the second than gain
alone.

The compressor's own match-length histogram says in advance where
selection can act. On children's stories (TinyStories) long matches
cover under 0.4\% of tokens; no induction head forms in any arm and the
score correctly predicts that it would not.

\section{What this involved}
Everything was built from scratch in Python with PyTorch and
TransformerLens: two synthetic task generators with independently
controllable data knobs, a training and checkpointing harness, exact
direct-logit-attribution decomposition, activation patching and greedy
sufficient-set analyses, the compressor scores, the greedy selector,
and a streaming pipeline for The Stack on rented GPUs. Each synthetic
task was mechanism-traced before it was swept; three shortcuts that
would have produced clean-looking results about the wrong mechanism
were caught this way, and the induction task was redesigned three
times. About 200 training runs on a laptop and eight on a single rented
RTX 4090; the whole real-data study cost about \$7.

\section{Limits and next}
Models of 2--4 layers, one circuit family, one learning rate, a
formation window early relative to production budgets. Not yet shown:
selection acting on prose, and a circuit where the hand-written scorer
should fail and the learned one take over (facts with paraphrases),
which is the planned second paper.
